# Public-health interface evidence audit

This is a supplementary source audit for the optional executive/public-health reference, not the scope of either general skill.

Checked the design claims below on 28 September 2026 against primary official guidance and original studies. This is a targeted claim audit, not a systematic review or a guarantee that every proposed pattern is correct in every setting.

## Findings that should change the skill

1. **Distinguish guidance, empirical findings, and design proposals.** “Action → meaning → evidence,” MONITOR/PREPARE/ACT NOW, six risk dimensions, two temporal modes, eight components, 16:9 layout, “one interaction away,” and the 60-second north star are design hypotheses/synthesis. The sources support underlying principles; they do not establish these exact designs as best, universally effective, or government standards. Preserve the report's existing qualifications and attach them wherever these patterns are reused. Treat the 60-second target as an evaluation starting point, never an operational deadline or validated success criterion.

2. **Do not implement the Decision Uncertainty Toolkit as “choose the smallest percentage.”** Wiggins et al. (2025) present a methods toolkit with a hypothetical worked example and workshop/conference feedback; this is not a controlled test demonstrating improved executive decisions. Its risk measure includes the magnitude and duration of policy-target failures. A policy with a higher probability of exceeding capacity can have lower cumulative downside. The paper also notes that simulation runs may need unequal weights and warns against directly comparing risk values from different models. Cite it as a method informing the design, and include the relevant loss/objective and time horizon rather than only threshold-crossing probability. [Original PLOS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0332522).

3. **Keep hypothetical model counts separate from event probabilities.** “63 of 100 model runs” describes the runs. Its interpretation as a real-world 63% chance requires justified probabilistic sampling/weighting and a defensible model. A chosen scenario set is not automatically a probability distribution. Show model/version, scenario assumptions, outcome, geography/population, horizon, and limitations. If a probability is unsupported, say it is unavailable; do not invent one to satisfy the template. This is a statistical/design safeguard, consistent with the simulation-weighting caveat above.

4. **Label every synthetic example locally.** Only the options table explicitly labels its percentages illustrative. The 63/100 claim, 37% chart, 38/14/8 mockup, days 8/12/14, jurisdiction counts, lab-return times, reporting completeness, and 48-hour revision window also need a visible “synthetic example” label. The 37% figure alongside a “central estimate” crossing on Day 12 is underspecified: define mean versus median, the interval type, and whether probability is for any crossing by a stated date. If central means the pointwise median crossing a fixed threshold, 37% probability of *any* crossing in that same period would be inconsistent. Prefer placeholders until a coherent dataset generates all labels.

5. **Do not prewrite the conclusion about uncertainty.** The example phrases “does not change the recommendation,” “unlikely to eliminate the need,” and “does not change the immediate protective action” must be conditional fields requiring assessment evidence. A template must also support “could change the action,” “decision sensitivity unknown,” or “assessment required.” Missing evidence does not establish low risk or justify a MONITOR state.

6. **Recommendation and selection must differ.** The report advises against silent approval defaults but its mockup preselects targeted preparation with a filled radio symbol. Display a recommendation label with no selected decision; record explicit authorized selection. Do not infer government authority, operational thresholds, deadlines, or approval from a design template.

## Useful additional empirical evidence

- **Numerical uncertainty is different from low-quality evidence.** Van der Bles et al. (2020), five experiments totaling 5,780 participants including a BBC field experiment, found small trust decreases, mostly for verbal uncertainty; numerical ranges had only minor effects. This supports openness but does not show an inevitable trust increase. [Original PNAS study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149229/).
- **Evidence-quality labels can change willingness to use information.** Schneider et al. (2022), three preregistered experiments totaling 6,489 UK participants, found low or ambiguous evidence-quality cues lowered perceived trustworthiness and intended use relative to no cue; high-quality cues were generally similar to no cue. These were public responses to COVID-19 case-fatality information, not measured policymaker performance. Keep calibration as the goal, not maximum trust, and test confidence labels with the intended audience. [Original Judgment and Decision Making paper](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/21/210525b/jdm210525b.html).

## Evaluation corrections

“Correct decision” cannot mean agreement with the skill's recommendation. For value-laden options, predefine acceptable decisions and quality criteria with authorized domain reviewers; use objective truth only when the scenario actually has one. Separate comprehension, policy judgment, evidence calibration, and procedural compliance. Zero observed errors in a small prototype study does not establish zero operational risk. The report's targets (“near-perfect,” “approaches 100%”) are aspirational until a sampling plan and acceptance criteria are defined.

Describe patterns derived from this report as an **evidence-informed design/review method**, with dated source notes and synthetic examples. Do not market it as a validated biological threat assessment tool, autonomous incident commander, or substitute for agency scientific and operational review.

## Minimal carry-forward rules

- Mark MONITOR/PREPARE/ACT NOW, the six risk dimensions, and the 60-second goal as **proposed design choices**, not an official taxonomy, validated performance threshold, or legal requirement.
- Keep agency authority, current policy, accessible task testing, qualified translation review where applicable, and source/handling boundaries in the workflow. Do not claim that generating a design produces compliance.
- Preserve publication date, evidence/data period, retrieval date, and effective/compliance date separately. Recheck time-sensitive law, policy, channels, and active advisories before operational use.
- Government case studies establish that patterns exist. They do not establish that copying those patterns improves executive decisions; that claim needs user/scenario evaluation.

## What this audit does not establish

This is not a systematic literature review, a complete reconstruction of every opaque original citation, or validation of the skill's effect on operational decisions. The report's unspecified medical-dashboard studies, NASA human-performance papers, particular warning experiments, data-modernization initiatives, and SUS attribution were not fully reconstructed. Their claimed effects are not carried into the skill as established facts. Exact current NTAS advisories and full FCC deadline text could not be directly retrieved. The report's illustrative numbers and timing are not data.

The core recommendation—start with the leader's decision and preserve inspectable evidence—is a defensible design synthesis. The prescribed component count, view count, ordering, chart layout, states, one-interaction access, fixed options, and timing targets remain choices to evaluate. Existing product requirements and actual user needs take precedence.

## Review and evaluation


Prioritize findings by the affected decision, likely consequence, and recoverability. Wrong authority, false completion, hidden evidence changes, misleading quantities, inaccessible commitment, or unauthorized disclosure can outweigh visual polish. Explain the observed behavior or missing evidence; a hypothetical failure is not a confirmed defect.

Use synthetic or approved scenarios with expected outcomes adjudicated by appropriate domain and operations owners. For value-laden policy choices, evaluate comprehension and the decision process rather than inventing a single correct policy. Include the user roles, handoffs, assistive technologies, language needs, data volume, and time pressure relevant to the task.

| Sequence or fault | What to verify |
| --- | --- |
| Weak initial evidence → corroboration with unchanged assessed risk | Users distinguish confidence improvement from worsening risk and can identify the actual action state. |
| Historical correction or backfill → new screen refresh | Change summary explains the correction; observation period and source age remain visible; no fabricated improvement or escalation. |
| Recommendation changes or credible sources disagree | Users can inspect the reason, compared assessment versions, uncertainty, and supplied alternatives without losing decision ownership. |
| Low likelihood/high consequence, with different confidence levels | Users understand the supplied assessment and approved next step; low confidence is not mistaken for low risk or permission to act. |
| Deadline arrives before evidence is resolved | The interface exposes the unresolved effect and responsible authority; it does not choose policy or auto-approve when a timer expires. |
| Material assessment update while an approval tab remains open | The pending commitment stays tied to a version; changed content is made explicit and the authorized workflow revalidates it. |
| Server records a decision but response is lost; dispatch partly fails | Reconciliation finds the original decision; no duplicate action is caused; recording, delivery, acknowledgement, and execution remain distinct. |
| Feed outage, stale snapshot, restricted source, or print/export | No false all-clear, freshness claim, or unauthorized disclosure; snapshot context survives the permitted export. |
| Source message changes after translation/review | Outdated translations and approvals are visible; publishing follows the actual version/approval rules. |
| Keyboard, screen reader, magnification, narrow screen, supported languages | Decision, caveat, owner, deadline, options, evidence, and feedback remain understandable and usable. |

Select relevant cases; a two-string copy edit does not require the entire suite. Test sequences because a user can understand a static screen yet misinterpret a revised assessment.

Measure correct comprehension of the situation, uncertainty, change, authority, and next step; critical errors; appropriate follow-through; evidence retrieval; recovery; and time to a correct task outcome. Compare with the actual current briefing process. Measure workload and usability as complementary signals, not substitutes for decision quality. NASA-TLX is a subjective workload instrument; follow its method when using it and do not infer an actual human score from an agent simulation. [NASA TLX](https://www.nasa.gov/human-systems-integration-division/nasa-task-load-index-tlx/)

Do not turn a small study with no observed failures into a zero-risk claim, promise a universal latency target, or use acknowledgement rate alone as evidence of useful alerts. Set thresholds with the accountable team and record the study conditions. For a deployed system, review near misses, misunderstood changes, ignored/false alerts, and after-action findings using proportionate data collection that respects handling rules.
